50cc Cafe Racer Motorcycle

A Powertrain Integration to a Restored Bicycle

This project began with the goal of creating a small, low-cost vehicle compliant with Alberta moped laws, that manifested itself into a 50cc cafe-racer styled motorized bike, inspired by early motorcycles.

Using a modified Honda GXH50, this bike is capable of speeds road tested to 80 km/h.

What began as a personal project quickly became an opportunity to practice mechanical design and fabrication, to study engine mechanics, and problem-solve to create a cohesive working vehicle.

Platform and Assembly

The platform of the build is a 1970s Free Spirit steel bicycle, which I restored prior to its useage in this project. Restoring the original frame provided an opportunity to inspect and adapt the components to ensure they could handle increased speeds and additional loads with the motorized conversion. After acid-batheing the components to remove rust and grease accumulation, cleaning and re-greasing the bearings, and retuning the brakes and gears, the bike was fit for the installation of the engine.

To integrate the engine components into the constraints of an existing frame, reiterative modifications and adjustments were required throughout the process of the build. The engine itself was mounted inside the frame using cast-iron joints and a sheetmetal base. The addition of the engine came with the installation of a rag-jointed rear sprocket, centrifugal clutch, throttle cable, kill-switch, fuel tank, and lights.

View of the engine

Performance Tuning

To optimize performance, various adjustments were made. The carbeurator was tuned to run a lean gasoline-air mix; while providing less power at low rpm, this configuration provides higher top end power at high rpm. The air intake and the exhaust were modified to allow greater airflow, lessening resistance on the engine, and the overhead valves were also loosened to allow more breathing room at a high rpm. To compensate for the loss of low end power, the centrifugal clutch springs were stretched to improve engagement at low rpm.

Final Result

Costing under $600 and using parts available online and locally, the build balances perfomance with accessibility and serviceability. Parts were selected with replicability in manufacturing and future replacement in mind, while retaining simplicity. Although the build relies on simple fabricated hardware and minor part modifications, this allows for replication by using lower cost, commericially available parts.

The completed build provided a hands-on experience with mechanical design and assembly where design decisions had immediate, observable effects on the performance of the vehicle. It was also an exercise in assembling interworking systems to create a coherent and working product.

Ultralight Twin-Motor RC Airplane

An Ultralight Airframe with Integrated Micro-Electronics

Constructed from foam board, balsa wood, and paper, this ultralight radio-controlled airplane has a gross weight of under 180 grams.

This plane utilises components sourced from a recycled drone. The constraints of the low-torque motors are reflected in the lightweight design through material choice and minimalist construction. Additionally, a dual-propeller design was conceptualised to retain maneouverability without the need of weight expenses in a moveable rudder, and the wings provide necessary lift to support flight without the need of ailerons.

The final design of this airplane was reached through a process of reiterative prototyping. Since this project was heavily constrained by the low power of the motors, 3 prototypes were studied before the final design, to perfect the weight balance and aerodynamics such that true flight could be supported.

MK4.5 Volkswagen Golf

As it stood post restoration

Repair and Restoration

What started as the purchase of a vehicle soon turned into a continuous project of restoration and maintenance. While initially driveable, this Volkswagen Golf was in no sense a perfect vehicle; mechanically and cosmetically it needed attention. In fact, when first purchased, it was taken to a mechanic for an inspection, who then quoted upwards of 4000$ for repairs. Refusing to pay nearly the amount I paid for the car itself in repairs, with an inclination to learn, I decided to do the work myself.

As it stood on day one

Mechanical Repair

The first work to be done was to return the vehicle to mechanical soundness. This work included full brake replacement, a front suspension rebuild, and rear shock replacement.

Note that at the time, I was not documenting my work with the intention of also making a portfolio. For this reason, many of the images I have of this project only detail the prior state of the vehicle, as a reference to how components were to be reassembled.

Initial state of the brakes

Initial state of the control arms

For their role in safety and handling of the vehicle, and their deteriorated state, I conducted a full replacement of the brakes and suspension assemblies. For the brakes, all rotors and pads were replaced, as well as the rear calipers. The rear shocks were replaced along with the front struts, control arms, and sway bar links. Rather than addressing each issue in isolation, this work was completed at once, navigating issues such as rust and narrow part fitment. The effects on the drive quality were immediate; braking felt solid, but most importantly, improved ability to remain stable in cornering and over rough surfaces came with the suspension rebuild.

Removing the front bumper

Painting the fenders

Restoring Aesthetics

After addressing major mechanical concerns, I began to work on cosmetic restoration. Around the front fenders, rust and corrosion was perpetuating through the paint, not only diminishing the car's visual appeal, but eventually threatening the structural integrity of the body.

Initial state

Restored state

The front bumper was removed to give access to the fenders, which were then removed for thorough restoration. The damaged areas were sanded to bare metal to remove existing old paint and rust before body filler was applied to restore the surface and volume of the fenders. The fenders were then primed, painted, and clear coated to restore them to their factory appearance. The same process was carried out on the side skirts, which had also developed rust and corrosion.

Removing the driver's door panel

Replacing the door sensor diode

The next problem addressed was the faulty driver's side door-open sensor, which was intermittently failing to register when the door was opened and closed. Removing the door panel provided access to the electronics, and exposed the diode that was responsible for an unreliable electrical current to the door sensor. As such, this component was replaced, restoring the door sensor to working order.

Dodge Dart PF

The Dodge Dart as it stands

Maintaining A Daily Driver

This Dodge Dart PF naturally is an ongoing project as I navigate maintainance, repairs, and upgrades throughout my ownership.

Since purchasing this vehicle in the summer of 2026, I have already completed some necessary mechanic and cosmetic work. Aside from continued maintenance, I soon plan to carry out a rear sway bar upgrade and front strut replacement to improve handling.

Initial Work Completed - Summer 2026

After purchasing this vehicle in summer 2026, a few immediate areas of concern were identified. These were addressed to retain mechanical fitness and cosmetic appearance.

Old Brakes

New Brakes

Array of Tools Used

Servicing the Brakes

To improve braking performance and stopping power, the four sets of original, worn-out pads and rotors were replaced. As such, this involved removing the old components, removing rust and debris accumulation from the hardware, and relubricating the necessary components prior to the installation of the new parts. Pictured is a deeply pitted old rotor, the fresh replacement rotor and pads on clean hardware, and the wide array of hand and pneumatic tools used to complete the repair.

The interior trim removed

Fixing the Handbrake

The handbrake was found to be non-functional at the time of the vehicle's purchase; it would not engage at all, rendering it useless for parking and emergencies. In an effort to restore its function, the entire interior trim, across the dashboard and into the glovebox had to be removed to access the handbrake assembly. It was found to have loosened with time, so it was tightened. The handbrake mechanisms on the rear brake calipers were also found to have been partially seized; they were broken loose with a hammer and relubricated. These two efforts together restored the function to the handbrake.

Repainting the Rims

For cosmetic reasons, I chose to repaint the rims to matte black, so that they would match the black and white aesthetic of the vehicle. For this process, the rust was old paint was first removed from the rims using a wire brush attachment to a drill. Next, the rims were painted with three coats of metal primer, and once dried, painted with four coats of matte black paint automotive paint. The paint was allowed to harden to an enamel, and the refreshed rims were then reinstalled on the vehicle.
